# Induction and Structural Induction

## One-Page Review

### Governing question

How can a finite derivation establish a property for every value generated by an infinite inductive definition, and how do we choose an induction hypothesis strong enough to cover recursive program structure?

### Essential definitions

- An **inductive definition** gives constructors or rules that generate the smallest closed set of values or judgments.
- **mathematical induction** proves \(P(n)\) for every natural number by proving a base case and a successor-preservation step.
- The **induction hypothesis** is the proposition assumed for structurally smaller or predecessor cases within the induction step.
- **strong induction** permits assuming \(P(k)\) for every \(k<n\) when proving \(P(n)\).
- **structural induction** follows an inductive datatype's constructors, supplying hypotheses for each recursive substructure.
- **rule/derivation induction** follows the last rule used to construct an inductively defined judgment, supplying hypotheses for premise derivations.
- A **motive** is the property family \(P(x)\) being proved by induction.
- **generalization** replaces overly fixed variables with universally quantified ones before induction so the hypothesis is reusable.
- A **well-founded relation** has no infinite descending chain and supports induction from all strictly smaller predecessors.
- A **counterexample** refutes a universal claim; finite checking supports examples and debugging but is not induction.

### Central model, mechanism, or theorem

Natural-number induction is

\[
\frac{P(0)\qquad \forall n\in\mathbb N.\ P(n)\to P(n+1)}
{\forall n\in\mathbb N.\ P(n)}.
\]

For lists generated by `nil` and `cons`, structural induction is

\[
\frac{P([])\qquad
\forall x,xs.\ P(xs)\to P(x::xs)}
{\forall xs.\ P(xs)}.
\]

The validity comes from the datatype being the least set closed under its constructors: base constructors are covered directly, and each recursive constructor preserves the property from its recursively contained values.

### Critical invariants

- State the domain, inductive generators, motive, and exact induction principle.
- Prove every base constructor and every recursive constructor/rule case.
- Use the induction hypothesis only for structurally smaller values or premise derivations authorized by the principle.
- Keep arbitrary variables arbitrary; do not fix them before induction when the step needs a generalized statement.
- Match recursive program calls with available induction hypotheses, or choose a different measure/principle.
- Distinguish case analysis from induction: cases enumerate constructors but supply no recursive hypothesis.
- Distinguish termination of recursive definitions from truth of an inductive theorem, even when both use the same structure.
- Record definitions, lemmas, axioms, simplification rules, tool/kernel versions, and admitted gaps for machine-checked proofs.

### Common failures

Common failures are omitting zero or empty cases, proving \(P(n)\to P(n+1)\) without proving a base, assuming \(P(n+1)\) in its own proof, using a hypothesis for a non-smaller value, checking many examples and claiming induction, proving only the recursive function's output examples, fixing an accumulator or second list too early, choosing induction on the wrong argument, confusing strong induction with a stronger conclusion, and hiding an unproved lemma behind automation.

### Compact example

For list append defined by `[] ++ ys = ys` and `(x::xs) ++ ys = x::(xs++ys)`, prove

\[
length(xs++ys)=length(xs)+length(ys)
\]

by structural induction on `xs`, generalized over `ys`. The empty case reduces definitionally. In the cons case, unfold append and length, apply the hypothesis to `xs` and the arbitrary `ys`, then simplify successor/addition. Induction on `ys` would not align with the recursive definition of append and would require extra lemmas.

## Governing Question

Why is it legitimate to prove infinitely many natural-number, list, tree, syntax, or typing cases by checking finitely many proof obligations?

The answer is not “the pattern continues.” Induction is an inference principle tied to how a domain is generated. Natural numbers are generated from zero by successor. Lists are generated from empty and cons. Binary trees are generated from leaves and nodes. Typing derivations are generated by inference rules. If a property holds for every base generator and is preserved by each recursive generator assuming it holds for recursive inputs, it holds throughout the smallest generated domain.

That same structure organizes recursive programs. A function over lists handles empty and cons cases; a proof about it often follows those cases and uses the hypothesis exactly where the function recurses. When the recursion and proof do not align, the proof may need a generalized theorem, an auxiliary lemma, strong induction, a measure, or induction over another object such as a derivation.

## Why This Matters

Programming-language theory is saturated with inductive objects: abstract syntax, evaluation relations, typing judgments, reductions, traces, environments, proofs, and data structures. Type safety proofs commonly induct over typing or evaluation derivations. Compiler correctness arguments induct over syntax or execution. Recursive algorithm proofs induct over input structure and use the defining equations.

Induction also prevents an evidence error common in engineering. Tests over sizes 0 through 10,000 can find counterexamples and build confidence, but they do not cover all natural numbers. A property-based generator samples or enumerates a bounded fragment; it does not supply the closure argument that induction supplies. Conversely, a valid theorem over a mathematical model does not prove that source code, numeric representation, parser, or environment implements that model. Proof and empirical connection are complementary evidence layers.

## Prerequisites and Retrieval

You should understand predicates, universal quantification, implication, equality, functions, recursive definitions, lists, trees, and natural-deduction assumptions. Retrieve these facts:

1. A universal proposition is refuted by one counterexample.
2. An implication step does not establish its antecedent.
3. A recursive call must progress toward a base case under a termination argument.
4. Constructors define possible shapes of an inductive value.
5. A derivation is a finite tree whose final node is justified by one inference rule.
6. An equality rewrite needs a previously established equality in the correct direction/context.
7. A theorem is relative to definitions, arithmetic, axioms, and observations.

## Core Exposition

Natural numbers can be characterized as the least set containing \(0\) and closed under successor \(S\). The leastness clause excludes extra unreachable objects. The induction principle says every predicate whose extension contains zero and is successor-closed contains all naturals. This is a property of the inductive definition, not a heuristic about observed sequences.

A natural-number proof has four explicit parts: proposition \(P(n)\), base proof \(P(0)\), arbitrary \(n\) with hypothesis \(P(n)\), and step proof \(P(n+1)\). The word **arbitrary** matters. The step cannot use a special fact about a chosen numeral unless that fact holds for every \(n\). After establishing the implication uniformly, the induction principle concludes \(\forall n.P(n)\).

As a first theorem, define

\[
sum(n)=\sum_{i=0}^{n}i.
\]

Claim \(P(n):2sum(n)=n(n+1)\). At zero, both sides are zero. Assume \(2sum(n)=n(n+1)\). Then

\[
2sum(n+1)=2sum(n)+2(n+1)
=n(n+1)+2(n+1)
=(n+1)(n+2).
\]

This is exactly \(P(n+1)\). Multiplying by two avoids a division convention; over mathematical naturals the familiar formula follows. Over fixed-width machine integers, overflow requires a different statement.

Starting at another lower bound changes the base. To prove \(P(n)\) for all \(n\ge n_0\), prove \(P(n_0)\) and preservation from each \(n\ge n_0\). An induction with base 1 does not establish a theorem at zero. For propositions split by parity or a recurrence using \(n-2\), use two bases or an induction principle that matches the recurrence.

**Strong induction** states that when proving \(P(n)\), one may assume \(P(k)\) for every \(k<n\). It proves no more natural-number propositions than ordinary induction under standard foundations; it supplies a more convenient hypothesis. Prime factorization existence, recursive division, and algorithms that call on varying smaller inputs align naturally with it. The step must still prove strict decrease and cover the smallest values.

Strong induction is a special case of **well-founded induction**. For relation \(\prec\) on domain \(A\), if no infinite descending chain exists and

\[
\forall x.\bigl((\forall y\prec x.P(y))\to P(x)\bigr),
\]

then \(P\) holds for all \(x\). A measure \(\mu:A\to\mathbb N\) induces a well-founded relation \(y\prec x\) when \(\mu(y)<\mu(x)\). Module TPROG-008 develops termination measures; here the important obligation is that every hypothesis use is justified by strict descent.

Structural induction generalizes natural-number induction. Define lists over \(A\):

\[
List(A)::=[]\mid x::xs
\]

where the cons constructor contains recursive substructure `xs`. To prove \(P(xs)\) for all lists, prove \(P([])\), then for arbitrary \(x,xs\), assume \(P(xs)\) and prove \(P(x::xs)\). There is no induction hypothesis for `x` because it is an element, not a recursive list argument.

For a binary tree

\[
Tree(A)::=Leaf(a)\mid Node(left,right),
\]

the node case receives two induction hypotheses, one for each recursive subtree. A proof using only the left hypothesis is permitted if sufficient, but both must be available. A datatype with several base constructors needs a case for each. An option type with `None | Some(a)` has no recursive occurrence, so its elimination is case analysis rather than a genuinely recursive induction.

Structural recursion and structural induction are aligned eliminators. A recursively defined `mirror` handles leaves directly and calls itself on both node children. To prove `mirror(mirror(t))=t`, structural induction supplies exactly `mirror(mirror(l))=l` and `mirror(mirror(r))=r` in the node case. Unfolding twice and rewriting with both hypotheses yields the original node.

Case analysis alone would expose `Leaf` and `Node(l,r)` but would not prove anything about `l` and `r`. This distinction matters for recursive types. A proof script command named `cases` may simplify a shallow property but cannot justify recursive claims without hypotheses. An `induction` tactic usually performs cases plus generated hypotheses according to a selected induction principle.

The induction motive must express enough information. Suppose `reverse` is defined with an accumulator helper. Attempting to prove only `reverse(xs)=...` may leave a step about the helper with an arbitrary accumulator. Strengthen the lemma to quantify over every accumulator, prove that generalized statement by induction, and instantiate it afterward. Stronger intermediate claims can be easier because their hypotheses are usable in more contexts.

Variable order matters in interactive proofs. For

\[
length(xs++ys)=length(xs)+length(ys),
\]

the statement should be \(\forall xs.\forall ys.P(xs,ys)\), and induction on `xs` should retain arbitrary `ys`. If `ys` is introduced and fixed in a way that the step later needs a different tail or accumulator, the hypothesis can be too specific. Generalize dependent variables before induction.

Induct on the argument that drives recursion. Append normally recurses on its first list, so induction on `xs` makes its defining equations reduce. Induction on `ys` is logically possible for some theorem, but the expression `xs++(y::ys)` does not unfold when `xs` is unknown. One may need an auxiliary lemma, creating avoidable work. Read the function definition before selecting the induction variable.

Auxiliary lemmas are part of proof design, not failure. Proving `reverse(reverse(xs))=xs` for a simple reverse definition often needs

\[
reverse(xs++ys)=reverse(ys)++reverse(xs)
\]

and append associativity. The main induction stalls because the induction hypothesis cannot rewrite through an unproved interaction. Identify the mismatched expression, formulate the smallest reusable relationship, prove it independently, then resume.

Program syntax is inductive. For arithmetic expressions

\[
e::=Const(n)\mid Var(x)\mid Add(e_1,e_2),
\]

a structural proof has constant, variable, and addition cases, with two hypotheses in the last case. A transformation such as variable renaming or constant folding can be proved to preserve size, free-variable relationships, or evaluation by matching its recursive clauses. Binding forms require capture-avoiding definitions and stronger lemmas, developed later.

Judgments defined by inference rules support **derivation induction**. Suppose big-step evaluation \(e\Downarrow v\) has one derivation constructor per evaluation rule. To prove every evaluated expression satisfies property \(Q(e,v)\), inspect the last rule. Each premise derivation supplies an induction hypothesis. The proof covers all derivations because the judgment is the least relation closed under its rules.

Derivation induction is not the same as induction on expression syntax. One expression can have several derivations in a nondeterministic semantics, or the property may depend on intermediate values found only in premises. Conversely, a syntax induction may be appropriate for a property quantified over every environment even before an evaluation derivation exists. Choose the object that contains the recursive evidence needed by the proof.

Typing judgments provide a central example. A proof of weakening often inducts over the typing derivation because each last typing rule identifies premise derivations and context operations. Type preservation may induct over an evaluation derivation and invert a typing derivation, or vice versa, depending on the semantics. “By induction” is incomplete unless it names the induction object and principle.

Mutually inductive syntax or judgments can require mutual induction. If expressions contain lists of expressions and definitions recurse mutually over expressions and lists, the proof hypotheses may also need to be established together. Nested, indexed, and dependent inductive families generate more precise principles with equality constraints. This chapter establishes the constructor/rule method; proof-assistant modules later examine generated eliminators.

Induction proves truth, not termination of arbitrary proof search. A structural proof term is finite because it uses an eliminator justified by the inductive object. Automation can choose cases, simplify equations, discover hypotheses, and invoke lemmas, but the kernel should check the resulting proof. A tactic timeout does not refute the theorem; a successful tactic does not justify omitted axioms or incorrect definitions.

Proof auditing starts from the elaborated theorem. Confirm quantifiers, types, definitions, arithmetic, imports, axioms, admitted holes, generated induction principle, simplifier lemmas, and trusted kernel. Then inspect each constructor/rule case and hypothesis use. Finally connect modeled functions and data to implementation artifacts with extraction, refinement, tests, or independent review. A proof of the wrong model can be flawless.

## Definitions and Notation

Let \(F:\mathcal P(A)\to\mathcal P(A)\) be the monotone operator adding all values constructible from a candidate set by an inductive definition. The inductive domain is the least fixed point

\[
\mu F=\bigcap\{X\subseteq A\mid F(X)\subseteq X\}.
\]

If predicate extension \(P^*=\{x\mid P(x)\}\) is **closed under constructors**, meaning \(F(P^*)\subseteq P^*\), then leastness gives \(\mu F\subseteq P^*\). This is the general closure explanation for structural induction.

Natural induction uses constructors

\[
zero:Nat,\qquad succ:Nat\to Nat.
\]

List induction for motive \(P:List(A)\to Prop\) has premises

\[
b:P([]),\qquad s:\forall x,xs.\ P(xs)\to P(x::xs).
\]

Tree induction for `Leaf(a)` and `Node(l,r)` has premises

\[
\forall a.P(Leaf(a)),
\]

\[
\forall l,r.\ P(l)\to P(r)\to P(Node(l,r)).
\]

For judgment \(J(x)\) generated by rules \(R_i\), a rule-induction motive can depend on the complete judgment indices. Each rule case assumes the motive for recursive premise derivations and proves it for the rule conclusion.

Well-founded induction over \((A,\prec)\) is valid when every nonempty subset has a \(\prec\)-minimal member, equivalently under standard classical conditions when no infinite descending sequence exists. Exact equivalences depend on foundations, so a proof assistant's `WellFounded` definition is authoritative for a mechanization.

## Mechanisms, Invariants, or Proof Structure

**Natural induction soundness intuition.** Let \(S=\{n\mid P(n)\}\). The base puts zero in \(S\); the step makes \(S\) successor-closed. Since naturals are the least zero-containing successor-closed set, every natural belongs to \(S\).

**Append-length proof.** Induct on `xs`, leaving `ys` arbitrary. Base: `length([]++ys)=length(ys)=0+length(ys)`. Step: assuming `length(xs++ys)=length(xs)+length(ys)`, unfold to

\[
length((x::xs)++ys)=1+length(xs++ys)
=1+length(xs)+length(ys)
=length(x::xs)+length(ys).
\]

The rewrite uses the hypothesis only on recursive sublist `xs`.

**Mirror involution.** Leaf is immediate. For `Node(l,r)`, double mirror unfolds to `Node(mirror(mirror(l)),mirror(mirror(r)))`. Rewriting with both subtree hypotheses gives `Node(l,r)`. Constructor injectivity is implicit in equality congruence.

**Strong-induction minimal-counterexample argument.** If the strong step holds but some \(P(n)\) fails, choose a least failing natural \(m\). Every \(k<m\) satisfies \(P(k)\), so the strong step gives \(P(m)\), contradiction. This classical presentation exposes the well-ordering foundation.

**Generalization diagnostic.** If the induction hypothesis mentions one fixed \(y_0\) but the recursive step needs the property at transformed \(g(y_0)\), it cannot be instantiated. Prove \(\forall y.P(x,y)\) by induction on \(x\) so the hypothesis can be used at any \(y\).

**Derivation coverage.** Every finite derivation has a last rule. A case for each rule plus hypotheses for all premise derivations proves the motive for the conclusion. This assumes the judgment has no unlisted axioms/rules and is interpreted inductively rather than coinductively.

**Finite-check boundary.** The executable checks 21 naturals, nine list pairs, and five trees. It can expose mismatches in definitions or examples. It cannot establish closure for values not enumerated, so the proof evidence is the base/constructor reasoning above.

## Alternatives and Trade-Offs

**Ordinary versus strong induction.** Ordinary induction offers one immediate predecessor hypothesis. Strong induction offers every smaller case and aligns with nonuniform descent. They are equivalent in proving power over naturals but differ in proof convenience and obligations.

**Structural versus measure induction.** Structural induction follows immediate subterms and matches structural recursion. Measure induction supports algorithms whose recursive calls are smaller by size but not literal subterms. A measure introduces an extra strict-decrease proof.

**Syntax versus derivation induction.** Syntax induction exposes constructors of expressions. Derivation induction exposes semantic/typing rule premises and intermediate judgments. Select the evidence needed; sometimes a proof nests both.

**Weak versus strengthened theorem.** The narrow theorem may be the desired API statement but have an unusable hypothesis. A generalized accumulator, environment, or context lemma strengthens the induction and is instantiated afterward. Stronger statements can enlarge proof work and should remain motivated.

**Manual versus automated induction.** Manual cases make the principle and hypotheses visible. Tactics reduce boilerplate, choose generated principles, generalize indices, and simplify equations. Automation must emit a checkable proof and the handoff must record axioms, generated obligations, and unresolved goals.

**Proof versus exhaustive finite decision.** For a genuinely finite domain, exhaustive checking can decide the proposition and may be simpler than induction. For unbounded recursive domains, enumeration is calibration, not proof. A verified decision procedure can itself be justified by induction.

**Inductive versus coinductive reasoning.** Induction covers finitely generated data/derivations and least fixed points. Infinite streams and ongoing behaviors use greatest fixed points and coinduction/bisimulation. Applying an induction hypothesis to an unproduced infinite tail is not automatically sound.

## Failure Modes and Misconceptions

1. **“The first several cases establish the pattern.”** They suggest a conjecture; induction supplies closure for all generated cases.
2. **“The induction hypothesis is an extra axiom.”** It is a scoped premise authorized only while proving the successor/constructor case.
3. **“Assume the theorem for n+1 and prove it.”** That is circular unless another well-founded principle justifies the assumption.
4. **“A base case is optional if the step is obvious.”** A successor-closed property can still fail everywhere without an initial member.
5. **“One base case always suffices.”** Recurrences stepping by two or domains with several base constructors require matching bases.
6. **“Case analysis on a tree is structural induction.”** Cases alone provide no hypotheses for child trees.
7. **“Induct on the largest-looking variable.”** Induct on the recursive definition or relation that exposes needed hypotheses.
8. **“A stronger induction hypothesis makes strong induction.”** Strong induction refers to predecessor availability; theorem strengthening is a separate proof-design move.
9. **“Automation found the proof, so assumptions are irrelevant.”** Imports, axioms, generated eliminators, simplifier lemmas, and admitted holes remain part of the claim.
10. **“Structural recursion proves the function correct.”** It helps prove termination; functional correctness needs a separate property proof.
11. **“A theorem over naturals applies to machine integers.”** Overflow, bounds, undefined behavior, and representation require refinement.
12. **“Induction over syntax covers every semantic behavior.”** Nondeterministic or environment-dependent derivations may require rule induction and additional invariants.
13. **“A failed induction means the theorem is false.”** The motive, variable order, induction object, or auxiliary lemmas may be wrong.
14. **“Machine checking proves the real requirement.”** It proves an elaborated formula relative to definitions, axioms, kernel, and model connection.

## Worked Examples

### Example 1

**Question:** What exactly is assumed in the successor step for the sum formula?

**Reasoning:** For arbitrary \(n\), assume only \(2sum(n)=n(n+1)\). Use the recursive equation `sum(n+1)=sum(n)+(n+1)` and algebra to derive the formula at \(n+1\). The theorem at \(n+1\) is the goal, never an assumption.

**Conclusion:** The induction hypothesis concerns the predecessor selected by the principle.

### Example 2

**Question:** Why does proving `P(n) -> P(n+1)` not prove every `P(n)`?

**Reasoning:** Let \(P(n)\) be false for every natural. Then the implication from false to false is true for each \(n\), yet no \(P(n)\) holds. A base case supplies the first member from which closure propagates.

**Conclusion:** Preservation without initialization is insufficient.

### Example 3

**Question:** When is strong induction the natural choice?

**Reasoning:** To prove every integer \(n\ge2\) is prime or a product of primes, a composite \(n=ab\) has factors \(2\le a,b<n\), not necessarily \(n-1\). Strong hypotheses apply to both factors. Smallest cases and the composite split must be explicit.

**Conclusion:** Strong induction matches recursive dependence on arbitrary smaller values.

### Example 4

**Question:** How does the append-length proof know it covers every list?

**Reasoning:** Empty and cons are the only constructors. The base covers empty. The step shows any cons list has the property if its tail does. Every finite list is built by finitely many cons applications from empty.

**Conclusion:** Constructor coverage plus leastness supplies universal structural coverage.

### Example 5

**Question:** Why can a proof of double reverse require a lemma about append?

**Reasoning:** Unfolding reverse on a cons commonly produces `reverse(xs) ++ [x]`. Reversing that result needs a rule for reverse over append. The main hypothesis speaks only about `reverse(reverse(xs))`, so it cannot rewrite the larger interaction until the append lemma is established.

**Conclusion:** A stalled step often reveals a missing algebraic interface between recursive functions.

### Example 6

**Question:** What hypotheses arise for `Node(left,right)` in a tree proof?

**Reasoning:** The constructor has two recursive tree arguments, so structural induction provides \(P(left)\) and \(P(right)\). Nonrecursive labels or payloads are arbitrary parameters but receive no induction hypotheses.

**Conclusion:** Induction hypotheses correspond exactly to recursive constructor positions.

### Example 7

**Question:** Should preservation of an expression transformation be proved by induction on output size?

**Reasoning:** If the transformation recurses by input syntax constructors, syntax induction on the input exposes each defining equation and hypotheses for transformed children. Output size may change unpredictably and obscure the semantic cases.

**Conclusion:** Align the induction object with the recursive computation and semantic specification.

### Example 8

**Question:** Why induct over a typing derivation rather than just the expression?

**Reasoning:** A typing property depends on the final typing rule, its context, selected type, and premise typings. The derivation records that evidence. Syntax alone may admit expressions with no typing or several typings under different rules/contexts.

**Conclusion:** Rule induction follows evidence-producing judgments.

### Example 9

**Question:** What does the executable sample add to the proofs?

**Reasoning:** It checks exact definitions against 21 numbers, nine list pairs, and five representative trees. A mismatch would refute the encoded claim for a sample and reveal implementation errors. Passing does not cover unenumerated values; constructor proofs do.

**Conclusion:** Bounded execution is calibration and counterexample search, not universal induction.

### Example 10

**Question:** A verified infusion-pump state-machine controller uses induction over command traces. What must reviewers establish beyond a checked theorem?

**Reasoning:** Formal-method reviewers must inspect the trace datatype, transition rules, induction principle, invariants, arithmetic/units, axioms, admitted gaps, toolchain, and proof digest. Software reviewers must connect extracted or implemented code to the model and test hardware interfaces, timing, faults, concurrency, and updates. Clinical and human-factors reviewers must validate dosage requirements, alarm behavior, workflows, and foreseeable misuse. Safety/quality reviewers must trace hazards, independence, configuration identity, and deployment controls. This chapter claims no such external review.

**Conclusion:** Induction can close every modeled trace case while model adequacy and deployment safety remain separate obligations.

## Exercises

### Exercise 1

Prove by natural induction that \(\sum_{i=0}^{n}(2i+1)=(n+1)^2\). State the motive, base, induction hypothesis, and every algebraic step.

### Exercise 2

For lists, prove `map(f, xs ++ ys) = map(f, xs) ++ map(f, ys)` by structural induction. Explain why induction on `xs` aligns with both append and the recursive map definition.

### Exercise 3

Define binary-tree functions `size` (number of all constructors) and `mirror`. Prove `size(mirror(t)) = size(t)` by structural induction and identify every induction hypothesis in the node case.

### Exercise 4

A proof attempt for `reverse(xs ++ ys) = reverse(ys) ++ reverse(xs)` introduces both lists, then inducts on `ys` and stalls. Diagnose the choice, restate a useful quantifier order, and outline the corrected proof including any append lemma required.

### Exercise 5

Prepare a proof and validation plan for a parser theorem: every successfully parsed expression has balanced delimiters and its pretty-printed form reparses to an equivalent AST. State induction objects, necessary lemmas, error/recovery scope, mechanized evidence, executable evidence, hostile cases, and human-review boundaries.

## Answers to the Exercises

### Answer 1

Let \(P(n)\) be \(\sum_{i=0}^{n}(2i+1)=(n+1)^2\). Base \(n=0\): the sum is 1 and \((0+1)^2=1\). For arbitrary \(n\), assume \(\sum_{i=0}^{n}(2i+1)=(n+1)^2\). Then

\[
\sum_{i=0}^{n+1}(2i+1)
=\sum_{i=0}^{n}(2i+1)+(2(n+1)+1)
=(n+1)^2+2n+3
=n^2+4n+4
=(n+2)^2.
\]

This is \(P(n+1)\); induction concludes the theorem for every natural \(n\).

### Answer 2

Induct on `xs`, leaving `ys` and `f` arbitrary or fixed only if they never need transformation. Base: `map(f, [] ++ ys)=map(f,ys)=[] ++ map(f,ys)`. Step: assume `map(f, xs ++ ys)=map(f,xs)++map(f,ys)`. Then

`map(f,(x::xs)++ys)` unfolds to `f(x)::map(f,xs++ys)`, rewrites by the hypothesis to `f(x)::(map(f,xs)++map(f,ys))`, and folds to `map(f,x::xs)++map(f,ys)`. Both append and map inspect the first list, so the constructor case exposes their recursive calls.

### Answer 3

Define `size(Leaf(a))=1`, `size(Node(l,r))=1+size(l)+size(r)`, `mirror(Leaf(a))=Leaf(a)`, and `mirror(Node(l,r))=Node(mirror(r),mirror(l))`. Induct on `t`. Leaf is immediate. In the node case the hypotheses are `size(mirror(l))=size(l)` and `size(mirror(r))=size(r)`. Unfolding gives

\[
size(mirror(Node(l,r)))
=1+size(mirror(r))+size(mirror(l))
=1+size(r)+size(l)
=1+size(l)+size(r)
=size(Node(l,r)),
\]

using both hypotheses and commutativity/associativity of natural addition.

### Answer 4

Append and reverse of append expose the first list, so induction should be on `xs`, with theorem ordered \(\forall xs.\forall ys.\ reverse(xs++ys)=reverse(ys)++reverse(xs)\). Base reduces to a right-identity fact for append: `reverse(ys)=reverse(ys)++[]`. In the cons step, unfold append and reverse, apply the hypothesis to `xs,ys`, and use append associativity to regroup `(reverse(ys)++reverse(xs))++[x]` as `reverse(ys)++(reverse(xs)++[x])`. Prove right identity and associativity by structural induction on their first list if not already available.

### Answer 5

Separate two claims. Delimiter balance can be proved by induction over the successful parser derivation or returned CST, provided every construction rule records matching delimiters and recovery nodes are excluded or given a separate weakened property. Round-trip equivalence is naturally proved by structural induction over the AST: each constructor case relies on hypotheses for child ASTs plus lemmas about printer precedence, associativity, escaping, whitespace, lexical boundaries, and parser determinism. Binding-sensitive equivalence may require alpha-equivalence rather than tree equality.

Mechanization must pin grammar, lexer, parser/printer definitions, start/end goals, recovery policy, Unicode/encoding, generated induction principles, axioms, admitted holes, prover/kernel version, and proof digest. Executable property and differential tests should cover every constructor, nesting depth, empty/minimal forms, precedence/associativity, comments/trivia policy, escaped delimiters, malformed/truncated input, resource limits, and randomized AST generation; they calibrate implementation/model correspondence rather than replace proof. Named compiler/parser reviewers must assess implementation refinement and diagnostics, security reviewers must assess hostile inputs and denial-of-service, and domain owners must approve any DSL meaning. No external review or universal production correctness is claimed.
