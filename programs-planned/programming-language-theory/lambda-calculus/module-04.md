# Module 4 – Böhm Trees, Solvability, and Separation

From Module 3 we know:

* $\beta$-reduction is confluent (Church–Rosser),
* $\beta$-normal forms (if they exist) are unique,
* many $\lambda$-terms do **not** have $\beta$-normal forms.

In this module we move to an **infinitary** viewpoint: even when a term does not normalize, its observable **head behavior** can still be organized and compared.

Key notions:

* **Head reduction** and **head normal forms** (HNFs),
* **Solvable** vs **unsolvable** terms,
* **Böhm trees** as infinitary, canonical “normal forms”,
* **Lévy–Longo trees** as a finer infinitary semantics,
* **Böhm separation**: different Böhm trees $\Rightarrow$ contextual distinguishability.

Throughout:

* We work modulo $\alpha$-equivalence.
* We use $\beta\eta$-reduction as in Module 3.
* We are strategy-agnostic (no evaluation strategy is fixed here).

Application is written by **juxtaposition**: $t\, s$ means application of $t$ to $s$.

---

## Core 4.1 – Head Reduction, Head Normal Forms, and Solvability

We formalize what it means to “see the head” of a term. The definition of head normal form adopted here aligns with the standard treatment in Böhm-tree theory: a term is in HNF only if its body (after any leading $\lambda$-prefix) is neutral (variable-headed). This is consistent with the stricter notion emphasized in Module 3 (e.g., $\lambda x.\Omega$ is **not** an HNF, as $\Omega$ is not neutral).

### 4.1.1 Head contexts and head reduction

We only contract $\beta$-redexes that sit on the **head spine**: repeatedly follow applications on the **left**. We **do not** go under $\lambda$-abstractions. This ensures that $\lambda$-prefixes remain fixed under head steps, and reductions affect only the left spine of the body.

#### Definition 4.1 (Head contexts)

A **head context** is a one-hole context $H[\cdot]$ generated by:

$$
H ::= [\cdot] \mid H\, t
$$

where $t$ ranges over $\lambda$-terms.

So:

* we may add arguments on the **right** (applications),
* we never place the hole under $\lambda$,
* we never place the hole in the right argument of an application.

#### Definition 4.2 (Head $\beta$-reduction)

The **head $\beta$-reduction** relation $\to_h$ is defined by:

$$
H[(\lambda x.t)\, s] \to_h H[t[s/x]],
$$

for any head context $H$.

We write $\to_h^*$ for the reflexive–transitive closure of $\to_h$.

By construction, $\to_h$ is a **subrelation** of $\beta$-reduction: every head step is a $\beta$-step, but not conversely. More precisely, any $\beta$-step whose redex occurs within a head context position is a head step.

---

### 4.1.2 Head normal forms

We now characterize the absence of head redexes. The key is that the body must expose a variable as head, with no further $\beta$-redex on the left spine.

#### Definition 4.3 (Neutral terms and head normal forms)

A **neutral term** is generated by:

$$
q ::= y \mid q\, t
$$

where $y$ is a variable. Equivalently, a neutral term has the shape

$$
y\, t_1 \dots t_k \quad (k \ge 0)
$$

with $y$ a variable.

A term $t$ is in **head normal form** (HNF) if it has the shape

$$
t = \lambda x_1.\dots.\lambda x_n.\, q
$$

for some $n \geq 0$ and neutral term $q$.

Equivalently: an HNF is a (possibly empty) block of leading $\lambda$’s, followed by a neutral term. In particular, **the head is a variable**, not an abstraction. Every $\beta$-normal form is an HNF, but not conversely (arguments of the neutral head may still contain redexes). If the body after the $\lambda$-prefix is not neutral (e.g., an abstraction), the term is **not** in HNF, even if no head redex is present.

---

### 4.1.3 Solvable and unsolvable terms

We take the modern, standard definition, which aligns with the historical “solvable” notion (a term is solvable if it behaves like a function in some applicative context; see below).

#### Definition 4.4 (Solvable and unsolvable terms)

A term $t$ is **head normalizing** if there exists an HNF $u$ such that

$$
t \to_h^* u.
$$

A term $t$ is **solvable** if it is head normalizing.

A term that is not head normalizing is called **unsolvable**.

We will use this as the baseline notion of solvability. Note that solvability concerns only the term's own head behavior under $\to_h$; internal divergence in arguments (as in neutral bodies) does not affect it.

---

### 4.1.4 Equivalence with the applicative formulation

Solvability was originally formulated using **closed head normal forms** obtained by applying $t$ to fresh variables. This applicative view captures the idea that solvable terms “act like functions” when given enough arguments.

#### Definition 4.5 (Applicative solvability)

A term $t$ is **applicatively solvable** if there exist variables $x_1,\dots,x_k$ ($k \geq 0$) such that

$$
t\, x_1 \dots x_k \to_\beta^* u
$$

for some **closed** head normal form $u$ (i.e. $\mathrm{FV}(u) = \emptyset$).

#### Theorem 4.1 (Equivalence of solvability notions)

For any term $t$, the following are equivalent:

1. $t$ is solvable in the sense of Def. 4.4 (head-normalizing).
2. $t$ is applicatively solvable in the sense of Def. 4.5.

*Proof sketch (standard fact; see Barendregt [1984], §14.2 for details).* 

* (1 $\Rightarrow$ 2) If $t \to_h^* u$ where $u = \lambda x_1.\dots.\lambda x_n.\, y\, u_1 \dots u_m$ is an HNF, apply $t$ to fresh variables $z_1,\dots,z_n$ (avoiding free variables in $t$). Head steps on $t$ lift to $\beta$-steps on the application $t\, z_1 \dots z_n$, yielding a form $y\, (u_1[z_1/x_1 \dots z_n/x_n]) \dots (u_m[\dots])$. To close it, embed in a context that substitutes fresh constants for free variables $y$ and any frees in the $u_i$ (using $\eta$-expansion if needed). This yields a closed HNF via confluence.

* (2 $\Rightarrow$ 1) This direction relies on standardization and an analysis of how $\beta$-steps on $t$ lift to $\beta$-steps on $t\, x_1 \dots x_k$. One shows that any reduction to a closed HNF can be permuted into a sequence that first performs head steps on $t$, exposing an HNF $w$ with $t \to_h^* w$. Details appear in Barendregt [1984, §14.2].

∎

From now on, “solvable” means **head-normalizing** (Def. 4.4).

---

### 4.1.5 Examples

1. $I \triangleq \lambda x.x$

   Already an HNF ($n = 1$, neutral body $x$). Hence solvable.

2. $K \triangleq \lambda x.\lambda y.x$

   HNF: $\lambda x.\lambda y.x$. Solvable.

3. $\Omega \triangleq (\lambda x.\, x\, x)\,(\lambda x.\, x\, x)$

   The head is an application whose left term is a $\lambda$-abstraction:

   $$
   \Omega = (\lambda x.\, x\, x)\,(\lambda x.\, x\, x) \to_h \Omega \to_h \Omega \to_h \dots
   $$

   Head reduction loops forever, so $\Omega$ is **unsolvable**.

4. $\lambda x.\Omega$

   Head reduction does **not** go under $\lambda$. The body $\Omega$ is an abstraction (not neutral), so $\lambda x.\Omega$ has no head redex but is not an HNF (body not neutral). Thus it is **unsolvable**.

5. $\lambda x.\, z\, \Omega$ with free variable $z$

   Already an HNF: body $z\, \Omega$ is neutral (head $z$). The argument $\Omega$ diverges, but this does not affect the head. Hence **solvable**.

6. $(\lambda x.y)\, \Omega$

   The head redex is the outermost application:

   $$
   (\lambda x.y)\, \Omega \to_h y,
   $$

   and $y$ is an HNF (neutral variable). So this term is solvable (and even weakly normalizing, as seen in Module 3).

These examples show:

* Unsolvable $\neq$ “never terminates in any context”: for instance, $(\lambda x.y)\, \Omega$ terminates even with an unsolvable argument.
* Solvability is about the **term’s own head behavior**, not arbitrary contexts.

## Core 4.2 – Böhm Trees: Infinitary Normal Forms

Finite $\beta$-normal forms often do not exist; HNFs exist only for solvable terms. Böhm trees assign an **infinitary canonical object** to every term, capturing its head behavior and the head behavior of its arguments recursively.

From now on we work **modulo $\beta\eta$-equivalence** unless stated otherwise.

### 4.2.1 Böhm trees as infinite $\lambda$-trees

We treat Böhm trees as infinite $\lambda$-trees built from $\lambda$-prefixes and variable-headed spine nodes, plus a distinguished bottom element $\bot$.

#### Definition 4.6 (Böhm trees – coinductive shape)

The class of **Böhm trees** is given coinductively by:

$$
B ::= \bot \mid \lambda x_1.\dots.\lambda x_n.\, y\, B_1 \dots B_m
$$

with $n,m \geq 0$, variables $x_i, y$, and $B_i$ Böhm trees.

* $\bot$ represents **total head divergence**.
* Non-bottom trees record:

  * a $\lambda$-prefix,
  * a head variable,
  * Böhm trees of the arguments.

---

### 4.2.2 Böhm trees of $\lambda$-terms

We now associate a Böhm tree to each $\lambda$-term. Solvable terms yield non-bottom trees; unsolvable terms yield $\bot$.

#### Definition 4.7 (Böhm tree of a term)

Let $t$ be a $\lambda$-term (considered modulo $\beta\eta$).

1. If $t$ is **unsolvable** (Def. 4.4), define:

   $$
   \mathrm{BT}(t) \triangleq \bot.
   $$

2. If $t$ is solvable, then there exists a head normal form

   $$
   u = \lambda x_1.\dots.\lambda x_n.\, y\, u_1 \dots u_m
   $$

   such that $t \to_h^* u$. Define:

   $$
   \mathrm{BT}(t) \triangleq \lambda x_1.\dots.\lambda x_n.\, y\, \mathrm{BT}(u_1)\dots \mathrm{BT}(u_m).
   $$

Because head reduction is deterministic (unique HNF if it exists) and $\beta\eta$-equivalence preserves solvability and HNFs, this definition is **well-defined** up to $\alpha$-renaming.

---

### 4.2.3 Basic properties

#### Lemma 4.2 (Unsolvability and finiteness)

For any term $t$:

1. $\mathrm{BT}(t) = \bot$ iff $t$ is unsolvable.
2. $\mathrm{BT}(t)$ is **finite** (a finite $\lambda$-tree without any $\bot$ leaves) iff:

   * $t$ is solvable, and
   * for every HNF encountered along the recursive construction, each argument $u_i$ is solvable.

In particular, if $t$ is solvable then the **root** of $\mathrm{BT}(t)$ is not $\bot$.

*Proof.* Immediate from Def. 4.7 and Def. 4.4. ∎

#### Theorem 4.3 (Böhm trees respect $\beta\eta$-equivalence: soundness)

If $t \equiv_{\beta\eta} s$, then $\mathrm{BT}(t)$ and $\mathrm{BT}(s)$ are isomorphic as Böhm trees (up to $\alpha$-renaming of bound variables).

*Proof.* $\beta\eta$-equivalent terms have the same solvability status and (for solvable terms) the same HNF up to $\alpha$, by confluence, $\eta$-closure, and uniqueness of HNFs. The recursive definition of $\mathrm{BT}$ then gives isomorphic subtrees at arguments. ∎

Note that the converse fails: equal Böhm trees do not imply $\beta\eta$-equivalence (e.g., all unsolvables share $\bot$ but are not all $\beta\eta$-convertible). Böhm trees characterize a coarser theory, often denoted $H^*$ or the Böhm theory, which equates terms with identical head behaviors (including all unsolvables).

---

### 4.2.4 Examples

We write Böhm trees informally in $\lambda$/application notation.

1. $I \triangleq \lambda x.x$

   * HNF: $\lambda x.x$.
   * Tree: $\mathrm{BT}(I) = \lambda x.x$ (finite, no arguments).

2. $K \triangleq \lambda x.\lambda y.x$

   * HNF: $\lambda x.\lambda y.x$.
   * Tree: $\mathrm{BT}(K) = \lambda x.\lambda y.x$.

3. $\Omega \triangleq (\lambda x.\, x\, x)\,(\lambda x.\, x\, x)$

   * $\Omega$ is unsolvable.
   * $\mathrm{BT}(\Omega) = \bot$.

4. $\lambda x.\Omega$

   * $\lambda x.\Omega$ is unsolvable.
   * $\mathrm{BT}(\lambda x.\Omega) = \bot$.

5. $\lambda x.\, z\, \Omega$ (with free $z$)

   * HNF: $\lambda x.\, z\, \Omega$.

   * Tree:

     $$
     \mathrm{BT}(\lambda x.\, z\, \Omega) = \lambda x.\, z\, \mathrm{BT}(\Omega) = \lambda x.\, z\, \bot.
     $$

   * This shows that **internal divergence in arguments** is collapsed to $\bot$ while the head structure $\lambda x.\, z\cdot$ is preserved.

6. $(\lambda x.y)\, \Omega$

   * $(\lambda x.y)\, \Omega \to_h y$.
   * HNF: $y$.
   * Tree: $\mathrm{BT}((\lambda x.y)\, \Omega) = y$.

These examples illustrate that Böhm trees:

* distinguish solvable from unsolvable terms via $\bot$,
* preserve the **head $\lambda$-prefix and head variable**,
* collapse divergent argument structure to $\bot$ at the appropriate subtrees.

## Core 4.3 – Lévy–Longo Trees and Comparison

Böhm trees focus on **head normalization at each argument**. Lévy–Longo trees refine this picture by unfolding argument positions via weak head normal forms (WHNFs), which allow abstractions as heads in recursive steps (but not at the top level for pure terms).

### 4.3.1 Weak head normal forms

We slightly relax the HNF notion for recursive unfolding.

#### Definition 4.8 (Weak head normal form)

A term is in **weak head normal form** (WHNF) if it has the shape

$$
\lambda x_1.\dots.\lambda x_n.\, u
$$

where:

* $u$ is either a variable, or
* an application $v\, t_1 \dots t_m$ with $v$ **not** an abstraction.

Equivalently: there is **no $\beta$-redex on the head spine** (under head contexts), but there may still be redexes among the arguments $t_i$ or deeper inside $\lambda$-bodies. For pure $\lambda$-terms under $\to_h$, top-level HNFs and WHNFs coincide; the difference arises recursively in arguments.

---

### 4.3.2 Lévy–Longo trees

Lévy–Longo trees mirror the Böhm-tree construction, but use **weak head normalization** at each recursive step (still via $\to_h$).

#### Definition 4.9 (Lévy–Longo tree)

The **Lévy–Longo tree** $\mathrm{LL}(t)$ of a term $t$ is defined coinductively (modulo $\beta\eta$) as follows:

1. If $t$ is **unsolvable** (no WHNF reachable by head reduction $\to_h$), define:

   $$
   \mathrm{LL}(t) \triangleq \bot.
   $$

2. Otherwise, reduce $t$ by head reduction to a WHNF

   $$
   u = \lambda x_1.\dots.\lambda x_n.\, y\, u_1 \dots u_m
   $$

   with $y$ a variable. Define:

   $$
   \mathrm{LL}(t) \triangleq \lambda x_1.\dots.\lambda x_n.\, y\, \mathrm{LL}(u_1)\dots \mathrm{LL}(u_m).
   $$

LL-trees differ from Böhm trees in how they unfold argument positions recursively under WHNFs, not in the root shape.

---

### 4.3.3 Comparison with Böhm trees

#### Lemma 4.4 (Basic comparison BT vs LL)

For any $\lambda$-term $t$:

1. If $\mathrm{BT}(t)$ is finite (and has no $\bot$), then $\mathrm{LL}(t)$ is finite and coincides with it up to $\alpha$-renaming.
2. If $\mathrm{BT}(t) = \bot$, then $\mathrm{LL}(t) = \bot$.
3. There exist terms $t, s$ such that $\mathrm{BT}(t) = \mathrm{BT}(s)$ but $\mathrm{LL}(t) \neq \mathrm{LL}(s)$; thus LL-trees form a **strictly finer** observational semantics than Böhm trees.

*Proof idea.* (1) and (2) follow from the definitions: both use $\to_h$, with $\bot$ for divergence, but WHNFs in LL-trees do not alter solvability status. For (3), see Barendregt [1984], §15.3. ∎

We will not exploit LL-trees further; they indicate that Böhm trees are **not** the finest possible infinitary semantics.

---

## Core 4.4 – Böhm Approximants and Separation

Böhm trees act as canonical infinitary forms. The **Böhm separation theorem** states that distinct Böhm trees are contextually distinguishable (via head normalization). We discuss finite approximants and the separation result; the Böhm-out construction (a partial inverse from trees to terms) is sketched heuristically but not used in proofs.

### 4.4.1 Finite Böhm approximants

Finite approximants make precise the idea that Böhm trees arise as a **limit of finite observations**.

Define **depth** of a Böhm tree node in the usual way (root at depth 0; children at depth +1). For each natural number $k \geq 0$, define a **truncation operator** $\mathrm{trunc}_k$ on Böhm trees:

* $\mathrm{trunc}_0(B) \triangleq \bot$ for all $B$.
* $\mathrm{trunc}_{k+1}(\bot) \triangleq \bot$.
* $\mathrm{trunc}_{k+1}(\lambda \vec{x}.\, y\, B_1 \dots B_m) \triangleq \lambda \vec{x}.\, y\, \mathrm{trunc}_k(B_1)\dots \mathrm{trunc}_k(B_m)$.

#### Definition 4.10 (Depth-$k$ Böhm approximant)

For a term $t$, its **depth-$k$ Böhm approximant** is:

$$
\mathrm{BT}_k(t) \triangleq \mathrm{trunc}_k(\mathrm{BT}(t)).
$$

Then:

$$
\mathrm{BT}_0(t) \sqsubseteq \mathrm{BT}_1(t) \sqsubseteq \mathrm{BT}_2(t) \sqsubseteq \dots
$$

forms an **increasing chain** in the obvious pointwise order ($\bot \leq$ any tree; extension by replacing $\bot$ with more structure). Their supremum is exactly the full Böhm tree:

$$
\mathrm{BT}(t) = \bigsqcup_{k \in \mathbb{N}} \mathrm{BT}_k(t).
$$

This is the syntactic analogue of approximating elements in a domain by finite elements.

**Example.** For $\Omega$ we have $\mathrm{BT}(\Omega) = \bot$, hence $\mathrm{BT}_k(\Omega) = \bot$ for all $k$. For $\lambda x.\, z\, \Omega$ with $\mathrm{BT}(\lambda x.\, z\, \Omega) = \lambda x.\, z\, \bot$, we get $\mathrm{BT}_0(\lambda x.\, z\, \Omega) = \bot$ and $\mathrm{BT}_k(\lambda x.\, z\, \Omega) = \lambda x.\, z\, \bot$ for all $k \geq 1$.

---

### 4.4.2 Böhm-out (heuristic sketch)

The **Böhm-out** construction is a partial inverse to the Böhm-tree map: given a Böhm tree $B$, build a term $\mathrm{bo}(B)$ such that $\mathrm{BT}(\mathrm{bo}(B)) = B$. For finite trees, this is straightforward (mirror the structure). For infinite trees or $\bot$, encode recursion via fixed-point combinators (e.g., a $Y$-combinator from Module 5), ensuring head reduction unfolds to match $B$. Böhm-out realizes coinductive syntax in the $\lambda$-calculus but requires care with variable capture and regularity. We treat it as a heuristic; separation relies on approximants, not Böhm-out.

---

### 4.4.3 Böhm separation theorem

We now state the main result: Böhm trees provide a sound and separating semantics for head behaviors.

#### Theorem 4.5 (Böhm trees and $\beta\eta$ – soundness and separation)

Let $t, s$ be $\lambda$-terms. The following hold:

1. **Soundness**: If $t \equiv_{\beta\eta} s$, then $\mathrm{BT}(t) \cong \mathrm{BT}(s)$ (isomorphic up to $\alpha$-renaming). *(This is Theorem 4.3.)*
2. **Separation**: If $\mathrm{BT}(t) \not\cong \mathrm{BT}(s)$, then $t \not\equiv_{\beta\eta} s$. *(Contrapositive of soundness.)*

*Proof of separation.* Immediate from soundness.

A stronger form extracts contextual separation.

#### Corollary 4.6 (Contextual separation)

If $\mathrm{BT}(t) \not\cong \mathrm{BT}(s)$, then there exists a $\lambda$-context $C[\cdot]$ such that:

* $C[t]$ and $C[s]$ are both **solvable**, and
* $C[t] \not\equiv_{\beta\eta} C[s]$ *(in fact, $\mathrm{BT}(C[t]) \not\cong \mathrm{BT}(C[s])$ by soundness).*

*Proof idea.* Compare finite approximants $\mathrm{BT}_k(t)$ and $\mathrm{BT}_k(s)$ until they differ at depth $k$. Construct $C[\cdot]$ using Church encodings (e.g., for booleans or pairs) to “probe” up to depth $k$, forcing distinct HNFs (hence distinct BTs) for $C[t]$ and $C[s]$. Both remain solvable by preserving head structure. Full details are standard (see Barendregt [1984], §15). ∎

In particular, **distinct Böhm trees are observationally distinguishable** by some $\lambda$-context.

## Core 4.5 – Böhm Trees, Observational Equivalence, and Models

We indicate how Böhm trees relate to observational equivalence and denotational models, distinguishing three key relations:

- $\equiv_{\beta\eta}$: Convertibility in the least extensional $\lambda$-theory.
- $\approx$: Head-observational equivalence (below).
- $\equiv_{\mathrm{BT}}$: The relation $t \equiv_{\mathrm{BT}} s :\Leftrightarrow \mathrm{BT}(t) \cong \mathrm{BT}(s)$ (up to $\alpha$).

Write $\equiv_{\mathrm{BT}}$ for the equivalence relation $t \equiv_{\mathrm{BT}} s :\Leftrightarrow \mathrm{BT}(t) \cong \mathrm{BT}(s)$. We have:

$$
\equiv_{\beta\eta} \subsetneq \equiv_{\mathrm{BT}} = \approx.
$$

In particular, $\beta\eta$ is strictly finer than the Böhm theory, while $\approx$ and $\equiv_{\mathrm{BT}}$ coincide by Lemma 4.7.

### 4.5.1 Observational equivalence via head normalization

Two terms are **observationally equivalent** if no program context can distinguish them via the chosen observable.

Fix the class $\mathcal{C}$ of all $\lambda$-contexts (built from $\lambda$ and application, with a single hole).

#### Definition 4.11 (Head-observational equivalence)

Define $t \approx s$ iff for all $C[\cdot] \in \mathcal{C}$:

$$
C[t]\ \text{has an HNF under $\to_h^*$}
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
C[s]\ \text{has an HNF under $\to_h^*$}.
$$

Böhm trees tightly capture this.

#### Lemma 4.7 (Böhm trees characterize head-observational equivalence)

$t \approx s$ iff $t \equiv_{\mathrm{BT}} s$.

*Proof.* ($\Rightarrow$) If $t \not\approx s$, Corollary 4.6 gives a separating context, hence distinct BTs. ($\Leftarrow$) Any $C[\cdot]$ transforms BTs via fixed operations (prepend $\lambda$s, plug as arguments); equal BTs yield equal transformed trees, matching solvability. ∎

Thus, in the pure $\lambda$-calculus with head-normalization as observable, Böhm trees provide a **fully abstract** infinitary semantics for $\approx$ (but not for the finer $\beta\eta$).

### 4.5.2 Domain-theoretic viewpoint (very brief)

In denotational semantics, a standard model solves $D \cong [D \to D]$ in domains. Each term $t$ denotes $\llbracket t \rrbracket \in D$; finite $\mathrm{BT}_k(t)$ approximate $\llbracket t \rrbracket$ from below, with $\mathrm{BT}(t)$ as the least upper bound. Böhm trees thus syntactically present denotations in such models. A formal connection appears in later modules.
