# Elements, Relationships, and Emergent Behavior

## One-Page Review

### Governing question

How do the identities, states, capabilities, and limitations of system elements combine through relationships to produce whole-system behavior—including properties and failures that no element possesses alone?

### Essential definitions

- A **system element** is a member of the system at the selected level of abstraction; it may be hardware, software, data, a person, procedure, facility, organization, service, or natural feature.
- A **relationship** is a typed dependency or interaction between elements. It may convey matter, energy, information, control, authority, responsibility, timing, constraint, trust, or influence.
- **structure** is the arrangement of elements and relatively persistent relationships; **configuration** identifies a particular realized set and version of them.
- **state** summarizes history relevant to future behavior; **behavior** is change or response over time; **function** is a transformation or effect an element or system is intended to perform.
- **composition** forms a larger system from elements and relationships; **decomposition** views a system as elements for a stated concern. These are modeling acts, not discovery of one uniquely correct hierarchy.
- An **interaction** is an occurrence in which elements affect one another through a relationship under conditions.
- A **resultant property** can be derived by straightforward aggregation, such as total mass. An **emergent property** arises from organization and interaction and is not attributable to one element in isolation.
- **weak emergence** is explainable or reproducible from lower-level rules, often only through execution or simulation; stronger philosophical claims are outside this engineering chapter.
- A **local rule** governs an element using locally available state; a **global property** describes the composed system.
- A **cascade** occurs when one state change alters conditions for other elements, causing a propagating sequence.

### Central model, mechanism, or theorem

Model a system at time $t$ as:

$$
\Sigma_t=(E,R,X_t,U_t,\Phi,C),
$$

where $E$ is the element set, $R$ typed relationships, $X_t$ element and relationship state, $U_t$ environmental inputs, $\Phi$ the coupled transition mechanism, and $C$ constraints. Whole-system behavior is generated by composition:

$$
X_{t+1}=\Phi(E,R,X_t,U_t,C).
$$

Knowing every isolated element response is insufficient when $\Phi$ depends on topology, concurrency, delay, shared resources, human adaptation, or environment. Evidence must therefore cover elements, relationships, integration states, scenarios, and whole-system outcomes.

### Critical invariants

- Every element and relationship is defined for a named system boundary, level, concern, scenario, and lifecycle stage.
- Relationships are typed and directional where appropriate; an unlabeled line is not an engineering contract.
- State, function, behavior, and physical/logical realization remain distinct.
- Composition preserves applicable conservation, safety, security, timing, identity, and responsibility constraints.
- Local conformance does not entail global adequacy unless the composition argument covers interactions and assumptions.
- Emergence is explained through a candidate mechanism and evidence; it is not a synonym for surprising or mysterious.
- Desired and undesired emergent behavior both require analysis.
- Decomposition must not hide shared resources, feedback, environment, common causes, or human/organizational relationships.
- Models state abstraction limits; omitted interactions can invalidate conclusions.
- Simulation or execution demonstrates behavior only for the modeled rules, parameters, initial states, and scenarios.

### Common failures

Failures include treating the bill of materials as the system model; drawing untyped arrows; assuming component certification composes automatically; confusing function with implementation; assigning a whole-system property to one component; calling every aggregate emergent; attributing an observed pattern to “complexity” without a mechanism; decomposing by organization and missing operational coupling; ignoring timing and ordering; omitting people and procedures; validating only nominal interactions; double-counting elements at mixed abstraction levels; and inferring real safety from a simulation of incomplete rules.

### Compact example

Three power modules each safely carry 60 units and share a 150-unit demand equally: 50 each. One module disconnects. The two survivors now receive 75 each, exceed their local limit, and disconnect; the system loses all supply. No module violates its specified local trip rule, yet topology, redistribution, and threshold behavior create a cascading system failure. System evidence must test contingencies and load-sharing dynamics, not merely each module at 50 units.

## Governing Question

A system cannot be understood by inspecting components independently. Interfaces introduce delay and interpretation; shared resources couple otherwise separate elements; operators adapt; redundant elements can share one failure cause; and local protective actions can damage the mission. How can engineers reason from elements and relationships to whole-system behavior without either reducing the system to a parts list or declaring emergence unknowable?

The practical answer is disciplined composition. Select a level and concern, identify element states and capabilities, type the relationships, define interaction and transition rules, state environmental conditions, then derive or observe system behavior across scenarios. When a property depends on this organization rather than one isolated element, treat it as emergent and test the proposed mechanism.

## Why This Matters

Engineering organizations often allocate work by element. Teams specify a sensor, controller, network, operator procedure, or supplier service and verify each against local requirements. Yet mission success, safety, security, usability, reliability, and performance commonly live at interactions. Passing component tests is necessary evidence, but it is not a proof of correct composition.

ISO/IEC/IEEE 15288:2023 defines a system as interacting elements and applies lifecycle processes recursively to systems and system elements. The INCOSE Systems Engineering Handbook, fifth edition, treats interfaces, architecture, integration, verification, and validation as connected lifecycle concerns. SEBoK distinguishes emergence and provides systems-thinking context. These sources support a durable claim: decomposition manages attention and work, but systems behavior must be re-established through composition.

Herbert Simon's “The Architecture of Complexity” explains how near-decomposability and hierarchy make complex systems analyzable without making interactions vanish. Ludwig von Bertalanffy's general-systems work challenged explanations based only on isolated parts. Donella Meadows shows how relationships, information flows, delays, and rules shape system behavior. These are lenses, not licenses to avoid precise domain models.

Emergence matters because it can be beneficial or harmful. Fault tolerance emerges from coordinated redundancy and detection. Traffic flow emerges from many drivers and controls. Organizational safety culture emerges from incentives, information, authority, and practice. Cascading outage, deadlock, bank runs, alarm fatigue, and discriminatory outcomes can likewise arise from individually plausible actions.

## Core Exposition

**Choose the level before naming elements.** At one level, an aircraft is an element in an air-transport system. At another, propulsion, flight control, crew, maintenance information, and structure are elements of the aircraft system. At a lower level, a controller contains processors and software components. Mixing aircraft, an individual software function, and a national regulator as peer components creates false relationships. Use nested views and explicit allocation between levels.

An element is not necessarily indivisible. It is treated as a unit for the current concern. A database service may be black-box for a business workflow and white-box for availability analysis. The criterion is whether internal detail can change the answer to the question. Record that abstraction assumption.

**Type relationships.** A relationship can express physical attachment, flow, communication, control, sequencing, synchronization, constraint, trust, ownership, responsibility, spatial proximity, or statistical dependence. Direction matters: a sensor reports state to a controller; the controller commands an actuator; the actuator changes the environment; the environment affects the sensor.

Each material relationship needs source, destination or participants, content, enabling condition, capacity, timing, ordering, units, quality, failure behavior, security/safety properties, owner, and version as appropriate. N-ary relationships may be more honest than pairs: a transaction can coordinate several parties under one protocol; a maintenance action relates asset, technician, procedure, tool, authorization, and environment.

**Separate structure from configuration.** Structure defines permissible arrangement or topology. Configuration identifies actual element instances, versions, parameters, connections, and baselines. Two systems can share architecture but behave differently because one uses older firmware, a different threshold, or another operator roster. Verification evidence must bind to configuration.

**Separate function from behavior.** A braking system's function is to decelerate safely on command. Its behavior includes response delay, pressure oscillation, noise, wear, fallback, and failure. A queue's function may be to buffer work; its behavior includes backlog growth and starvation. Requirements often specify desired functions while hazards arise from actual behaviors and interactions.

**Model state at the right granularity.** Let element $e_i$ have state $x_i(t)$. The system state vector $X(t)=(x_1,\ldots,x_n,r_1,\ldots,r_m)$ may include relationship state: connection established, queue depth, trust credential valid, mechanical clearance, or responsibility assigned. Ignoring relationship state makes topology changes and communication failures appear inexplicable.

The next state of one element can depend on neighbors and environment:

$$
x_i(t+1)=f_i(x_i(t),\{x_j(t):j\in N_i\},r(t),u(t)).
$$

The composition $\Phi$ of these local rules produces a global trajectory. Synchronous versus asynchronous updates can change the result. So can delay, message loss, priority, shared capacity, and nondeterminism. State the execution semantics before interpreting a trace.

**Distinguish aggregates from emergence.** Total mass is normally the sum of element masses; the aggregation rule does not depend on organization. Vehicle stability, network reachability, organizational responsiveness, and musical harmony depend on arrangement and interaction. An emergent property is observable at a higher level and cannot be assigned to one isolated element under the model.

This does not make emergence magical. For weak emergence, engineers propose a mechanism and reproduce the property through analysis, experiment, simulation, or operation. Some global properties are formally derivable; others are computationally expensive or sensitive to unknown conditions. “Emergent” names the composition problem, not an excuse to stop investigating.

**Trace cascades.** A cascade requires at least a triggering state change, coupling that changes other elements' conditions, local response rules, and propagation or stabilization. Protective actions can be locally correct: a power module trips to prevent damage; a service sheds load; a bank preserves liquidity; an operator silences an alarm. At system level, synchronized protection can remove capacity or information and worsen the mission.

The executable example models equal load sharing among identical capacity-limited modules. After one planned outage, survivors overload and trip in rounds. It is a pedagogical threshold cascade, not a power-grid, electrical, or safety simulation.

**Analyze shared resources.** Elements interact even without direct messages when they share CPU, power, bandwidth, memory, physical space, attention, budget, identity namespace, or failure-recovery staff. Contention, priority inversion, head-of-line blocking, and correlated overload emerge through the resource relationship. A diagram containing only functional calls misses them.

**Analyze timing and ordering.** Two locally correct actions can conflict if simultaneous or reordered. A controller reads a stale sensor after an actuator change; two services reserve the same scarce item; an operator follows a procedure based on an alarm that arrives late. Define clocks, causal order, deadlines, synchronization, and tolerance where behavior depends on them.

**Analyze human and organizational elements.** People adapt goals and behavior. A procedure can change workload; an alert threshold changes attention; an incentive changes reporting; responsibility split across organizations changes escalation. Humans are not unreliable components with fixed failure rates. Model cognitive work, authority, communication, training, staffing, and adaptation appropriate to the concern.

Organizational relationships can produce emergence. If every team optimizes its local service metric by rejecting difficult work, whole-system completion falls. If incident responsibility is split across vendors, rational contractual behavior can delay recovery. Local objectives are part of $\Phi$.

**Desired emergence requires engineering.** Redundancy alone does not yield reliability. Elements need failure independence, detection, switching, state synchronization, capacity, and recovery. Team autonomy alone does not yield organizational adaptability. It needs clear boundaries, information, decision rights, and coordination. Specify the interaction mechanism and test conditions.

**Undesired emergence requires containment.** Use margins, diversity, isolation, rate limits, backpressure, hysteresis, damping, independent protection, graceful degradation, monitoring, and operator control where the domain supports them. Each mechanism adds interactions and failure modes. For example, redundancy can introduce split-brain behavior or common-mode software faults.

**Recompose after decomposition.** Allocate responsibilities to elements, then build an integration argument: interface compatibility, sequence, data/state alignment, resource capacity, timing, failure behavior, configuration identity, and scenario outcomes. Verify relationships and system properties explicitly. Component certificates transfer only under their assumptions.

**Use multiple models.** Structure diagrams show arrangement; state machines show modes; sequence diagrams show interactions; equations show conserved quantities and dynamics; networks show topology; agent simulations show local rules; tables show responsibilities and interfaces. Align identities, versions, assumptions, and scenarios across models. Inconsistent model views are evidence of an unresolved system question.

## Definitions and Notation

Let $E=\{e_1,\ldots,e_n\}$ be elements selected for concern $c$ at abstraction level $l$. Let a typed directed multigraph $G=(E,R,\tau)$ represent binary relationships, where $\tau(r)$ gives relation type. Hyperedges may represent interactions among more than two elements.

Let $X_E(t)$ be element state and $X_R(t)$ relationship state. Then $X(t)=(X_E(t),X_R(t))$. Let $U(t)$ be environmental input and $P$ parameters/configuration. A deterministic teaching model uses:

$$
X(t+1)=\Phi(X(t),U(t);G,P).
$$

Real systems may be continuous, stochastic, nondeterministic, hybrid, partially observed, or controlled by several authorities. The notation is a starting abstraction, not a universal representation.

A property $p$ is **element-local** if it can be evaluated on one isolated element under declared conditions. It is **system-level** if evaluation requires composition. A **resultant** has a justified aggregation mapping such as $p(\Sigma)=\sum_i p(e_i)$. An **emergent property** depends materially on $R$, $\Phi$, or environment and is not reducible to that simple aggregation.

An **invariant** $I(X)$ is intended to hold for every reachable state in scope. Local invariants $I_i(x_i)$ do not necessarily imply a global invariant $I(X)$. A composition proof must establish compatibility conditions:

$$
(\bigwedge_i I_i)\land Assumptions(R,\Phi,U)\Rightarrow I.
$$

If the implication is unproved, integration evidence must not be described as automatic compositionality.

## Mechanisms, Invariants, or Proof Structure

Use this composition-analysis procedure:

1. **State the concern and level.** Name the system boundary, question, scenario, lifecycle stage, and abstraction rule.
2. **Identify elements and configurations.** Record role, state, capability, limitation, owner, version, and evidence.
3. **Type relationships.** Include flows, control, timing, resource, trust, authority, dependence, and common cause.
4. **Define local transitions.** State triggers, guards, actions, delays, nondeterminism, and failure response.
5. **Compose execution semantics.** Define scheduling, order, concurrency, environment, shared resources, and relationship state.
6. **Derive candidate global properties.** Separate aggregates, intended emergence, undesired emergence, and unknowns.
7. **Challenge scenarios.** Vary initial state, timing, topology, load, failure, human action, and environment.
8. **Collect multi-level evidence.** Verify elements and interfaces; integrate configurations; validate whole-system outcomes.
9. **Compare observation to mechanism.** Investigate counterexamples and alternative explanations rather than naming surprise “emergence.”
10. **Revise models and controls.** Update structure, interaction, margins, operations, or boundary and re-observe.

Five invariants guide review.

**Level invariant.** Peer elements belong to a coherent abstraction level or have an explicit cross-level mapping.

**Relationship invariant.** Every interaction-dependent claim traces to typed relationships with conditions, state, and ownership.

**Configuration invariant.** Evidence identifies actual element versions, parameters, topology, environment, and procedures.

**Composition invariant.** Local guarantees transfer only through stated compatibility assumptions and a system-level argument.

**Emergence invariant.** Every claimed emergent behavior has an observable system property, candidate mechanism, scenarios, and evidence limitations.

## Alternatives and Trade-Offs

**Reduction versus holism.** Isolated analysis enables precise component knowledge. Whole-system views expose interaction. Alternate between them: decompose to investigate, recompose to justify outcomes.

**Black-box versus white-box models.** Black-box behavior protects abstraction and supports contracts. White-box structure explains internal interaction and failure. Open only the detail needed for the concern, but do not hide assumptions needed for composition.

**Hierarchy versus network.** Hierarchies clarify ownership and decomposition. Networks represent cross-cutting and many-to-many relationships. Use both where shared resources, influence, or systems-of-systems behavior crosses the hierarchy.

**Analytical proof versus simulation.** Proof can cover all states under a formal model; simulation handles richer models but samples trajectories. Neither validates model fidelity alone. Combine analysis, tests, experiments, simulation, and operation according to claim risk.

**Central control versus local rules.** Central control can coordinate global objectives but creates latency, scale, and failure concentration. Local rules improve autonomy and resilience but can produce oscillation, unfairness, or cascade. Specify information and authority limits.

**Tight versus loose coupling.** Tight coupling improves coordinated timing and efficiency but propagates change and failure. Loose coupling isolates elements but introduces latency, stale information, duplicated state, and reconciliation. Choose by required behavior and failure containment.

**Uniformity versus diversity.** Uniform components simplify operation but share defects. Diverse implementations can reduce common cause but increase integration and maintenance complexity. Evidence must cover both independence and interoperability.

## Failure Modes and Misconceptions

1. **The system is the sum of its parts.** Organization and interaction change behavior.
2. **Every whole property is emergent.** Some properties have straightforward aggregation rules.
3. **Emergent means inexplicable.** Engineering seeks mechanisms and bounded evidence.
4. **A component diagram models behavior.** Static adjacency omits state, timing, conditions, and transition.
5. **Verified elements compose safely.** Their assumptions can conflict at interfaces and shared resources.
6. **Redundancy guarantees reliability.** Common causes, detection, switching, and capacity determine the result.
7. **People are external disturbances.** Human and organizational elements often produce the capability.
8. **Decomposition follows the organization chart.** Work ownership and operational coupling are different structures.
9. **More detail makes a better model.** Mixed levels and irrelevant detail obscure the question.
10. **Simulation proves the real system.** It establishes behavior of implemented model scenarios only.
11. **Local optimization improves the whole.** Coupling can make individually rational action globally harmful.
12. **One stable topology exists.** Reconfiguration, failure, maintenance, and evolution change relationships.

## Worked Examples

### Example 1

**Question:** Is total vehicle mass emergent in the same way as handling stability?

**Reasoning:** Given non-overlapping element masses under one reference, total mass is a resultant computed by addition plus any explicitly modeled consumables. Handling stability depends on mass distribution, suspension geometry, tire behavior, controller timing, road, speed, and driver input. Move the same masses and stability changes while total mass does not. Test the aggregation assumptions for mass and a coupled dynamic model for stability.

**Conclusion:** Total mass is normally resultant; stability is an interaction-dependent system property and therefore emergent at this level.

### Example 2

**Question:** Three individually safe power modules cascade after one outage. Trace the mechanism.

**Reasoning:** Initially 150 units divide to 50 per module, below each 60-unit limit. C disconnects. The sharing relationship reallocates demand to A and B: 75 each. Their local protection correctly trips at load above 60, leaving no source and 150 units unserved. The trigger is C's outage, coupling is equal redistribution, local rule is overcapacity trip, and the emergent property is total supply collapse. More modules help only if surviving capacity and control prevent overload.

**Conclusion:** The cascade arises from topology plus redistribution and threshold rules, not from one defective trip mechanism.

### Example 3

**Question:** Two services each meet a 100 ms latency requirement. Must their sequential workflow meet 200 ms?

**Reasoning:** Only under compatible definitions and zero omitted delay. Add network latency, queueing, serialization, retries, authentication, orchestration, and tail dependence. If each 100 ms is a 95th percentile measured independently, percentiles do not add into a guaranteed 95th percentile workflow bound. Correlated load can worsen both simultaneously. Define end-to-end population and measure the integrated path.

**Conclusion:** Local latency claims do not compose by arithmetic without distribution, dependency, and interface assumptions.

### Example 4

**Question:** Two redundant pumps each have 0.99 mission reliability. Is the pair necessarily more reliable?

**Reasoning:** Under independent failures, adequate switching, shared supply survival, and sufficient capacity, parallel reliability may improve. If both ingest the same contaminant, use one controller, or the dormant pump fails to start, independence is false. Switching can itself fail. Model common-cause events, detection, isolation, transition time, capacity, maintenance state, and mission duration before computing system reliability.

**Conclusion:** Redundancy creates potential; interaction and independence evidence determine the emergent reliability.

### Example 5

**Question:** Every support team closes its assigned ticket within target, yet customer incidents remain unresolved. How?

**Reasoning:** The organization decomposed work by ownership, while incidents cross identity, network, application, supplier, and data services. Each team can rationally transfer the ticket when its local component appears healthy. The handoff relationship has no end-to-end owner, shared hypothesis, or completion criterion, so local target compliance increases circulation. Model responsibility and evidence flows; add incident authority and a customer-outcome closure rule.

**Conclusion:** Organizational relationships can produce system failure even when every element meets its local metric.

### Example 6

**Question:** A controller and sensor pass isolated tests but oscillate when integrated. What should be inspected?

**Reasoning:** Trace sampling rate, filtering, controller gain, actuator response, transport delay, quantization, and physical inertia. A delayed observation may cause the controller to correct a past state, overshoot, observe the opposite error, and correct again. Each element can conform to its own static input/output range. Integration must test closed-loop dynamics across parameter and delay bounds. SYSENG-005 develops feedback analysis further.

**Conclusion:** Oscillation is a relationship- and timing-dependent behavior that isolated conformance tests cannot expose.

### Example 7

**Question:** Does adding an emergency alarm always improve safety?

**Reasoning:** The alarm adds detection, prioritization, presentation, interpretation, and response relationships. If frequent false alarms overload attention, operators silence or discount it; if authority is unclear, no one acts; if several alarms synchronize, the critical signal is masked. Test representative workloads, degraded modes, accessibility, training, escalation, and recovery. The alarm may improve one hazard while creating alarm fatigue or unsafe action.

**Conclusion:** A safety component produces value only through a functioning socio-technical response system.

### Example 8

**Question:** A routing rule sends every job to the currently shortest queue. Why can queues oscillate?

**Reasoning:** Agents observe delayed queue lengths at the same time and select the same apparently shortest destination. That queue becomes longest before the next observation, so the population switches again. The local rule is sensible for one agent; synchronized stale information creates herd behavior. Randomization, hysteresis, reservations, or better state distribution can damp the pattern, each with trade-offs.

**Conclusion:** Global oscillation can emerge from identical local optimization under delay and synchronized action.

### Example 9

**Question:** A simulation reproduces a crowd bottleneck. Has the real evacuation risk been validated?

**Reasoning:** The run shows the modeled agents, geometry, speeds, route rules, and initial state generate a bottleneck. Real behavior may include families, mobility aids, smoke, panic, staff authority, blocked exits, communication failure, and cultural response absent from the model. Calibrate against observations, test adversarial scenarios, inspect sensitivity, and obtain qualified safety/human-factors review. No external evacuation or regulatory review is claimed.

**Conclusion:** Simulation supports a candidate mechanism within its model; real-system validation requires fidelity and operational evidence.

### Example 10

**Question:** A software update changes only one element. Why rerun system evidence?

**Reasoning:** The update can alter timing, message order, resource use, failure response, security trust, operator display, data schema, or compatibility. Identify changed element behavior and traverse incoming/outgoing, shared-resource, common-cause, and responsibility relationships. Select regression and integration scenarios from affected system properties, verify configuration identity, and monitor rollout. A textual API match is insufficient if nonfunctional behavior changes.

**Conclusion:** Change impact propagates through relationships; element locality in the repository does not imply system-local consequence.

## Answers to the Exercises

### Answer 1

At city-service level, elements include bicycles, docking/locking stations, riders, operator staff, maintenance process, payment/identity service, relocation vehicles, streets, and municipal/supplier systems treated as internal or external according to the declared boundary. Relations include reservation and payment information, physical docking, rider control, battery/energy flow for electric bicycles, maintenance responsibility, spatial proximity, and relocation flow. States include bicycle available/reserved/in-use/faulted, dock capacity, account entitlement, and maintenance queue.

Functions include authorize rental, provide mobility, secure/return bicycle, detect faults, and rebalance stock. Behaviors include actual trip, failed unlock, rider rerouting, queueing, and repair delay. Total bicycle mass/count is resultant under stated inventory rules. Station starvation, neighborhood access disparity, rebalancing oscillation, congestion, and trust are emergent because they depend on demand, topology, policy, operations, and user interaction.

### Answer 2

Initially each carries $150/3=50$, below 80. After one outage, two survivors each carry $150/2=75$, still below 80, so no secondary trip occurs and demand remains served. To survive any one outage with equal sharing, demand $D$ must satisfy $D/2\le80$, hence $D\le160$. This is conditional on equal redistribution, both survivors available, no transient overshoot, independent failure, adequate connection capacity, and the trip rule exactly as modeled. It is not a real electrical assurance calculation.

### Answer 4

Central control can use a global map, coordinate routes, enforce fleet-wide priorities, and resolve contention, but depends on communications, current global state, compute capacity, and controller availability. Delay can make commands stale; one compromise or outage affects the fleet. Local control reduces dependence and reacts to nearby hazards, but each robot sees partial state and locally shortest routes can create congestion, deadlock, oscillation, or unfairness.

A robust design may combine centrally assigned goals/reservations with local safety envelopes and peer negotiation. Define right-of-way, safe stop, communication-loss behavior, spatial reservations, clock assumptions, operator override, and recovery. Simulate and physically test density, crossing, stale state, simultaneous failure, emergency access, adversarial agents, and human behavior. Neither topology is inherently safe; safety is an emergent property of information, rules, timing, mechanics, environment, and authority.
